## Core idea (2-3 sentences)

Sabri Suby breaks down six psychological mechanisms big brands use to increase conversions and order value — qualification/scarcity, free trials, decoy pricing, ecosystem design, friction removal, and free entry offers. His argument: conversion rate is shaped as much by the *structure* of the offer and buying process as by the offer's content, and most of these tactics can be applied "ethically" to small businesses.

## Frameworks and steps (his own names)

1. **The Application Funnel** — Replace "book a call" with "apply/claim a strategy session." Add a short pre-call questionnaire (not just budget/timeline — ask "what have you tried, what are you doing now?") so prospects feel accepted, not sold to.
2. **The Endowment Effect** — Let people experience the product with low risk (e.g., free/extended returns, free trials) so loss-aversion makes it harder to walk away than to buy.
3. **Decoy Pricing** — Build 3 tiers (entry, middle/decoy, premium) so the choice becomes "which package" not "buy or not." The decoy protects margins and anchors the premium.
4. **The Missing Piece Effect** — Map the prospect's current state to their "dream destination," then design your product ecosystem so each purchase creates the felt need for the next one (like IKEA showrooms selling a whole room, not one chair).
5. **The One-Click Effect** — Audit every step of your buying/checkout process and remove friction (e-signature tools instead of printed PDFs, one-click payment) since each extra step is a chance to lose the buyer.
6. **The Entry Offer Effect** — Create a free or break-even "splinter offer" (lead magnet) that is genuinely better than competitors' paid stuff, to get the first (hardest) yes and put the buyer "in motion" toward paid offers.

## Examples he gives

- Soho House: application + waitlist model, manufactures desire/exclusivity (no numbers given).
- YouTube Premium: 30-day free trial, then paywall + ads.
- McDonald's: medium drink priced near large to push large size; he claims (his number) **"30% of people"** take the premium option purely because it's offered.
- IKEA: showrooms sell full room settings, not single items — customer goes in for a $149 chair, ends up spending (his example) **~$3,000** on the room.
- Amazon: patented one-click checkout in 1999, held it ~20 years (his claim).
- Spotify: **"over 600 million users"**, **"over half"** (his claim, i.e. ~400 million) use it free, to seed paid conversion.
- Sabri's own claim about himself: generated clients **"over $7.8 billion in sales."**

## Use for an AI automation agency

- Turn "book a call" into "apply for an automation audit" with a short qualifying questionnaire (current tools, biggest bottleneck, what they've tried) — makes the call feel earned, and gives you real targeting info, not fake gatekeeping.
- Offer a genuinely low-risk first step — e.g., a free or cheap mini-audit/workflow map — so the prospect experiences your thinking before paying for the build (endowment effect, done honestly by actually delivering value).
- Price builds in three honest tiers (e.g., single-workflow automation, multi-workflow package, full ops automation) so prospects choose a scope, not just yes/no.
- Design your offer ladder deliberately: a small audit or single automation should reveal the *next* bottleneck it exposes — not manufactured, but because automations genuinely create adjacent needs (e.g., automate lead intake → now they need automated follow-up).
- Remove real friction: use e-signature/one-click proposal and payment tools (Proposify, PandaDoc, Stripe links) instead of PDFs and manual invoices — this is pure process improvement, no manipulation.
- Build one genuinely excellent free resource (e.g., a sharp teardown video of a prospect's current manual process, or a template Zap/workflow) as your entry offer — the "give away the secrets" principle applies directly to automation education content on X/LinkedIn.
- Make the free/low-cost offer good enough to prove competence, then let the natural next need (more automations, maintenance, scaling) create the upsell — don't force urgency that isn't real.

## Do not copy

- The $7.8B in client sales and "real campaigns with real money" framing is Sabri's own track record/credibility flex — a 3-person agency can't claim this and shouldn't invent comparable numbers.
- McDonald's/Amazon/Spotify/IKEA scale (hundreds of millions of users, decades of brand trust, patents) — these tactics work partly *because* of massive existing traffic/brand recognition a small agency doesn't have.
- Fabricated statistics like "30% take the premium option" — don't borrow or restate his unverified numbers as if they're general truths; don't manufacture your own stats to sound authoritative.
- Artificial scarcity/waitlists copied from Soho House-style brands with real exclusivity — a new agency faking a waitlist or application backlog it doesn't have is dishonest, not "qualification."
- Running a loss-leading entry offer at scale (like Spotify's free tier) requires funding to sustain unpaid users long enough for conversion — a small agency can't bankroll large-scale "free" at a loss the way VC-backed platforms do.
