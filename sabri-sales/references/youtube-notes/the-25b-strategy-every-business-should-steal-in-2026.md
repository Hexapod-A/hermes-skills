## Core idea (2-3 sentences)

Dyson doesn't sell vacuum cleaners — it sells a named, patented "hero mechanism" (cyclone technology) that reframes a commodity product as a proprietary category of one. Sabri Suby extracts five repeatable tactics from Dyson's playbook — naming your mechanism, demonstrating it live, using the founder as proof, comparing directly against competitors, and using one product as an entry point to a value ladder — that any business can apply to escape price comparison and justify premium pricing.

## Frameworks and steps

1. **Hero Mechanism Factory** — Find the pressing problem your market is "starving" to solve, look at what people complain about across existing solutions, then build/name a unique mechanism that solves it and center every sales touchpoint around that name (not generic features).
2. **Let the Demonstration Do the Selling** — Create an experience (showroom, website, demo video) where the prospect can personally try/see the mechanism work, so the product sells itself instead of relying on a salesperson's claims.
3. **Founder as Living Proof** — Put the founder front and center telling the origin story (the problem that enraged them, the obsessive work to fix it) because stories build identification and trust that generic brand claims can't.
4. **Comparative Advertising** — Study competitors' 1-star and 5-star reviews to learn exactly what the market loves/hates, then build your mechanism and messaging to explicitly lack those flaws, and show the side-by-side comparison — but only after you've verified your product is genuinely superior.
5. **Trojan Horse Strategy** — Pick the product/service with the largest addressable market as your "entry" hero product, make it a category killer, win the customer with it, then let that trust carry them up a value ladder to your other offers.

## Use for an AI automation agency

- Name your actual mechanism honestly: if you have a specific audit methodology or build process (e.g., "the 3-Layer Automation Audit"), name it and use that name consistently in emails, DMs, and the landing page — but only if it's a real, describable process, not just marketing fluff.
- Replace feature-dumping ("we build workflows in n8n") with problem-framing: identify what small-business owners are actually complaining about (missed leads, manual data entry, slow follow-up) and position your mechanism as solving that specific pain.
- Use demonstration over claims: short videos/Loom walkthroughs showing a real automation running live (before/after a lead comes in) do more selling than describing benefits in a cold email.
- Put a real founder story in cold outreach and content — the actual struggle of learning to build these systems, a real first client, real mistakes — since genuine specificity builds trust more than generic "we help businesses automate" copy.
- Use honest comparison, not trash-talk: research what SMB owners dislike about doing things manually or about generic SaaS tools, and show (with real screenshots/outcomes) how your build removes those specific frictions — never fabricate a vendor comparison you haven't verified.
- Only claim superiority you can prove: before pitching "this will save you X hours," actually test the automation and be able to show the real before/after numbers from a pilot or audit.
- Treat the first paid build (e.g., a lead-routing automation) as an entry point: deliver it well, then naturally earn the right to sell a broader retainer or additional automations — not by upselling tricks but by earning trust through one good result.
- Keep urgency and scarcity grounded in reality: no fake "only 3 spots left" — if there's a genuine capacity limit for new builds, say so; otherwise don't invent one.

## Do not copy

- Dyson's 200+ physical showrooms and $1,000+ price-anchoring theater require retail infrastructure and brand capital a small agency doesn't have — don't try to replicate the "immersive luxury showroom" experience literally.
- The patent moat (230+ patents) and years of R&D/engineering investment (5,127 prototypes) are not replicable by a tiny agency; don't claim proprietary "patented technology" for an automation workflow you didn't actually invent or protect.
- Large-scale comparative ad campaigns naming specific competitors (like "Say Goodbye to the Bag" vs. Hoover) carry legal/reputational risk and usually require big ad budgets and legal review — a small agency should stick to feature/approach comparisons, not attacking named competitors publicly.
- The claim that founder-story ads are proven across "thousands and thousands" of his own ads is his own agency's scaled ad-testing history — a small agency won't have that volume of data to validate messaging the same way; treat his specific numbers as his claims, not guaranteed results.
